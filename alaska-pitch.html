The core argument

Every family gets its own door — and all 20 share one world

Separate rooms for separate families is exactly right — nobody wants 20 people under one literal roof. The real question is what happens between the doors: how do 20 people spanning 65 years of age actually find each other, eat together, and still each have a good day? On land that means cars, group texts, and somebody always hosting.

A ship handles it automatically. Seven private cabins on the same corridor. Free kids' clubs by age band, a teen lounge, a covered pool, quiet decks for the grandparents — and every single night, two adjoining tables of ten at the same fixed dinner seating, no reservation scramble, no driving, no cooking rotation. The scenery moves so nobody has to.

Kid Freedom Report

Five kids, ages 6–11, off the leash all week — safely

This is the card no villa, hotel, or fjord town can play. A cruise ship is a contained world: no traffic, no open front door, crew on every deck, and nowhere to actually go except somewhere fun. The cousin pack — an 11, two 9s, two 6s — gets a week of pools, the arcade, the pizza and ice-cream counters, deck-top movies, and each other, on their own, in a way modern kids almost never get at home.

The honest rules: Princess's kids club lets ages 8–12 sign themselves in and out with parent permission — so the 9s and the 11-year-old genuinely come and go all week, while the 6-year-olds get full club days but need a parent pickup (a five-minute walk, not a car ride). And on Princess's MedallionClass ships, the wearable medallion shows each kid's live location on the ship in the parents' app — free-range with a safety net. Independence applies on sea days and evenings; in ports, everyone explores together.

For the 9s and the 11-year-old especially, this may be the single most memorable part of the trip: the week they got to be free.

The recommended plan

Princess "Voyage of the Glaciers" northbound + DIY Denali

Late June 2028 · Vancouver → Whittier (Anchorage), 7 nights · then 3 land days to Denali National Park. Princess and Holland America are the one-way Gulf of Alaska specialists; Princess's route is the only 7-night itinerary that includes both Glacier Bay National Park and College Fjord — two full scenic-cruising days — plus Ketchikan, Juneau, and Skagway. Northbound means the trip ends in Alaska, exactly where the Denali extension and the flights home begin.

The highlight reel

Five experiences the family will still talk about in 2048

1

Glacier Bay + College Fjord — two full glacier days included in fare

Park rangers board the ship; calving ice thunders off a 250-foot wall while all 20 of you stand at the same rail. The toddler needs nothing but a jacket; the teens will film it. This is the trip's beating heart, and it costs zero extra.

2

Juneau humpback whale watching $123–$230/person · ~$2,800–3,200 group

Near-100% sighting rates in peak season on heated, enclosed boats — genuinely toddler-safe and teen-approved. A group of 20 can often charter an entire small boat to itself.

3

Skagway: White Pass & Yukon Route Railroad $155 adult / $77.50 child · ~$2,300–2,600 group

A vintage narrow-gauge train climbing 3,000 feet over Gold Rush trestles and cliff-hugging tunnels, boarding steps from the pier — living history with knockout views that hooks everyone from the 3-year-old to the grandparents. The single best all-twenty-ages excursion of the trip.

4

Ketchikan: Lumberjack Show & totem poles ~$40–60/person · ~$1,000 group

Cheap, hilarious, one hour, steps from the ship. Teens who want more adrenaline can swap in a rainforest zipline (~$189).

5

Denali Tundra Wilderness Tour $144.75 adult / $66 child · ~$2,200 group

Grizzlies, caribou, moose, and Dall sheep from the park's classic wildlife bus, deep past where private cars can go. (Honest note: it's 6–7 hours — one parent may sit it out with the toddler at the lodge's free sled-dog demo.)

Full disclosure

The real pros and cons — including the ones that hurt

Why it works

  • Private doors, shared world. Every family keeps its own cabin, yet the whole group shares one corridor, one pool deck, and dinner for 20 at two adjoining tables every night — with zero driving and one unpack.
  • Real independence for the 6–11 crowd. A contained, crew-staffed ship lets the 9s and the 11-year-old roam club-to-pool-to-pizza on their own (Princess allows 8+ self-sign-out with parent permission, with live location on the parents' app) — freedom no land option can safely offer.
  • Every age has a lane. Free kids' clubs (ages 3–17 in bands), a teen lounge, a covered pool, quiet adult decks — parents actually get a vacation too.
  • Two included glacier days — the wow moments cost nothing extra and happen with everyone together at the rail.
  • 2028 timing is perfect: the grandkids will span 18 months to 16 — the big kids old enough to remember it forever, everyone still young enough to all come.
  • Cool-weather comfort: 60s–70s°F, 18–20 hours of daylight, no sunburned toddlers — energy left for long days of exploring.
  • Roughly half Disney's price for a better itinerary, and the land leg ends at Anchorage's airport — nonstop home to Salt Lake, direct West Coast service toward San Diego, one easy connection to Boston.

Where it genuinely hurts

  • Passports required for all 20, including the kids (one-way Vancouver sailings void the birth-certificate exception). A to-do, not a dealbreaker: ~$1,300–1,500 for the nine kids, applications on the calendar by late 2027.
  • The under-3 gap is real — it now touches two kids. The 18-month-old is below every mainstream line's kids-club minimum (age 3, fully potty-trained) and barred from all pools (swim diapers banned fleet-wide) — a parent is on baby duty whenever the others swim. And the 3-year-old makes the club cutoff only if fully potty-trained by June 2028. (Small bright spot: the 4-year-old clears the age-3 minimum and joins the club, potty-training permitting.) Workarounds exist but cost: Royal Caribbean has the sole mainstream paid drop-off nursery (6–36 months) for the baby, and Disney (2× the price) adds a swim-diaper splash zone.
  • Motion sickness risk is real. The final Gulf of Alaska crossing is open ocean and can roll noticeably. Mitigate with midship cabins and Bonine, but anyone badly prone to seasickness will feel it.
  • Open-jaw airfare costs ~$6,000–8,000 more than a simple round trip, and three regions converging on Vancouver demands a buffer day.
  • Weather is a coin flip. Juneau averages 64°F with regular drizzle; Denali's summit hides ~2 days out of 3. Pack rain shells, not swimsuits — there is no beach on this vacation.
  • $30k realistically buys only the lean version. The full recommended experience is $52–60k ex-air. Excursions for 20 add up fast ($6,500 for the full slate), and 7 connecting cabins require booking the day 2028 inventory opens.
The closing argument

Why Alaska wins the family vote

Greece is gorgeous — and it's ferries, heat, and four rental cars with nine kids. The Slovenian villa is charming — and someone cooks for 20 every night while the teens ask what there is to do. Maui is easy — and it's three condos, one pool, and a week that looks like every other resort week.

Alaska is the only pitch where the hotel itself does the traveling. Twenty beds move together past calving glaciers while the family sleeps, and every generation wakes up to the same jaw-dropping view — then splits into kids' club, teen lounge, whale boat, and quiet deck, and reunites at the same dinner table by seven.

The grandparents get front-row grandeur and a week fully off duty — nobody cooks, nobody drives, nobody hosts. The teens get whales breaching and a gold-rush railroad. The little ones get moose, sled dogs, and a ship to explore. The parents get actual rest. And in 2048, nobody will say "remember that villa?" — they'll say "remember when the glacier cracked and the whole family screamed at once?"

One booking window, one corridor of cabins, one unforgettable map line from Vancouver to Denali. That's the reunion. Vote Alaska.
